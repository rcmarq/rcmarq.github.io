Seawater Osmium Isotopes as a Tracer of South China Sea Evolution

The South China Sea is the largest marginal basin in the world, at the confluence of the Eurasian, Indo-Australian, and Pacific–Philippine Sea plates. Its Cenozoic history — marked by seafloor spreading, ridge jumps, and post-spreading magmatism — makes it a natural laboratory for testing whether basin-scale tectonics are recorded in seawater geochemistry.

We conducted the first Re-Os isotope study of South China Sea sediment cores from ODP and IODP drilling and Philippine exploration wells, covering both conjugate margins. Three negative Os isotope anomalies at ~35–33 Ma, ~26–23 Ma, and ~13–6 Ma correspond to distinct tectono-magmatic events: the onset of spreading, a ridge jump at the East Sub-basin, and post-spreading magmatic input from the Luzon Arc, respectively. The oldest anomalies also imply connectivity to global ocean circulation prior to the Oligocene.
